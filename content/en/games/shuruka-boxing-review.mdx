Shuruka Boxing shipped on Steam on June 2, 2025 with the boldest claim in the genre — "the most realistic first person boxing game ever" — and then spent the following year making the claim hard to argue with. This review covers what the game actually delivers: the adaptive AI that is its genuine innovation, the combat loop built on real boxing's dodge-block-punch rhythm, where it falls short of its own standard, and the player it was made for.

## The Claim and the Reality

Realism claims in first person boxing are usually marketing shorthand for "slower punch animations." Shuruka's version is structural. The game models a fight as an exchange of reads: opponents carry tendencies — preferred ranges, tell-tale windups, defensive habits under pressure — and the AI updates its behavior against yours as a fight progresses. Exploit the same opening twice and the third attempt meets a prepared guard. Fight flat-footed and the opponent takes the ring. It is the closest a flatscreen title has come to the experience of boxing a person rather than a pattern.

The physical layer supports the same ambition. Punches have commitment — thrown weight that must recover — and power scales with mechanics rather than input speed: a cross landed behind proper rotation hits like one, while an arm-punch from the same button does meaningfully less. Damage accumulates where it lands, stamina drains at rates that punish hero-combos, and the fight's tempo ebbs the way a real bout's does. None of this is perfectly implemented — stamina recovery still runs generous in later rounds, and body-work AI oscillates between neglected and sudden — but the systems are real, legible, and consequential.

## The Dodge-Block-Punch Loop

The core loop deserves its own section because it is where the realism lives minute to minute. Defense in Shuruka is not a stamina tax in front of the real game — it is the real game. Slipping a jab puts you in position to counter inside the opponent's recovery; blocking softens what it catches and sets up returns; leaning buys angles. The loop's discipline — engage, read, defend, punish — maps directly onto how boxing actually works, and the game's difficulty curve is essentially a curriculum in it: every opponent teaches the habit the previous one exposed you lacking.

That curriculum is also the review's main caution. Shuruka is demanding in a way arcade-leaning players experience as dry: rounds are paced, openings are earned, and fights are lost more often than they are thrown. Players who want immediate spectacle have an entire rest of the genre built for them — this is the title for the player whose favorite moments are tactical ones.

## Content and Structure

Around the fighting, Shuruka is honest about being a fighting game: a roster of opponents with distinct styles and habits, a progression of challenges that escalates the AI's aggression and craft, and the practice tools a simulation needs. What it deliberately does not offer is the life-sim layer — there is no open world, no gym economy, no story climb; Rising Spirit exists for that appetite, and comparing the two titles on career depth misses that they are different species of the same genre.

Where its out-of-ring investment went is polish: responsiveness (critical in a game whose defense runs on quarter-second reads), opponent variety, and the tuning pass that keeps later opponents hard in honest ways. Post-launch support has been the quiet strength — the update cadence through the game's first year is the kind that signals a studio with a plan, and the player discussions show it: AI quirks get found, reported, and patched.

## Performance and Price

On an ordinary gaming PC, Shuruka runs comfortably — it is a physics-and-AI game, not a spectacle game, and its demands track its genre rather than its ambition. Price sits in the standard indie band, and the verdict on value is simple: for the realism-first player it is the genre's best dollars-to-hours ratio, because the opponent AI is the content and it keeps generating fights that differ.

## Who Shuruka Boxing Is For

Buy it if: you want first person boxing that rewards study — real defense, real reads, opponents that adapt, and improvement you can feel transferring between sessions. Buy it if you tried arcade boxing and wanted the version where the ring teaches you something.

Skip it if: you want career narrative (Rising Spirit), chaos and mods (Ook Boxing), or timeless arcade charm (Beast Boxing Turbo). And if you own a VR headset and have not yet boxed in it — do that first; Shuruka is the best flatscreen answer to the genre's promise, but the promise itself is strongest in VR.

## FAQ

**Is Shuruka Boxing really the most realistic first person boxing game?** Among flatscreen titles, it has the strongest case on the market — adaptive AI and mechanics-scaled power are real simulation features, not marketing. VR sims match its realism with actual physics, which is a category advantage rather than a design failure.

**Is it beginner friendly?** It is beginner *teachable* rather than beginner *easy*. The early opponents are a genuine curriculum, but the game assumes you want to learn. Players who want immediate fun should start elsewhere in the genre.

**How is it on a gamepad versus mouse and keyboard?** Both control schemes are solid; the genre's input grammar is simple enough that neither is disadvantaged. Choose by comfort, not advantage.

**Does it have a career mode?** Not in the life-sim sense — progression is challenge-and-opponent structured. The career fantasy in this genre belongs to Rising Spirit.

**Is it still being updated?** Yes — the first year's patch cadence has been steady, with AI behavior the recurring focus. It is the rare realism claim that the developer keeps testing in public.
