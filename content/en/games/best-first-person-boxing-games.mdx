This is the full ranking — every notable title in the genre, flatscreen and VR, ordered by how well each delivers the genre's core promise: putting you inside the fight. ## 1. Shuruka Boxing — The Realism Standard

Shuruka Boxing arrived on Steam in June 2025 claiming to be the most realistic first person boxing game ever made, and the genre has largely agreed ever since. Its pillars are the three things sims always promise and rarely deliver: **dynamic AI opponents** that read your patterns and adapt mid-fight — lean on the same hook twice too often and the third one gets countered; a **dodge-block-punch loop** where defense is a genuine skill with genuine rewards rather than a stamina tax; and **physicality** — punches carry weight in both directions, and eating a clean shot from inside your own offense feels like the mistake it was.

The career framing is modest next to dedicated life sims, and that is the correct trade: Shuruka spends its budget on the fight. For players who want boxing — technique, adaptation, discipline rewarded — this is the flatscreen benchmark. Players who bounce off it almost always wanted a story instead of a sport.

**Verdict:** the buy for realism-first players, and the genre's best answer to "which game actually teaches boxing?"

## 2. Rising Spirit — The Career Fantasy

Rising Spirit is the other pole of the genre: a gritty first person boxing life sim set in a small open world, built around the climb from broke nobody to world champion. The fights matter — this is first person boxing, and the punches land — but the game around them is the point: training, money, the world between bouts, and a progression structure that makes each fight a chapter rather than an arcade level.

Its fighting model is less simulation-strict than the genre's purest sims, tuned instead for drama and stakes across a long climb. That tuning is a feature for its audience: career players want their fifteenth fight to feel different from their first because of everything that happened in between, and Rising Spirit delivers exactly that arc. Players who want to box for an hour and quit will not see what the fuss is about; players who want to become someone will not put it down.

**Verdict:** the buy for story-and-progression players, and the genre's best answer to "which game gives my fights meaning?"

## 3. The VR Library — The Full-Strength Experience

Ranked as a single entry because the platform earns it: VR is where first person boxing stops being a camera trick. Your arms are the controls, your defense is your actual head movement, and the genre's promise — you, in the fight — is fulfilled to a degree no flatscreen title can reach. The VR wing has quietly become one of the platform's most-recommended fitness categories, with players logging hundreds of sessions not out of discipline but because boxing in VR is the rare workout that is genuinely fun.

The trade-offs are the platform's: shorter comfortable sessions, a smaller premium on narrative depth, and hardware that costs more than any game here. Within the wing, titles divide by intensity — from technique-measured sims to cardio-first workouts to the physics-heavy experiences whose highlight reels ("VR Boxing Is Becoming WAY Too Violent," as one massively-viewed retrospective put it) drive the genre's ongoing virality. The VR guide breaks down the picks by purpose.

**Verdict:** the buy for anyone who owns a headset — start here before anywhere else in the genre.

## 4. Beast Boxing Turbo — The Arcade Classic

Beast Boxing Turbo is the 2013 ancestor that kept the genre warm through its lean years, and it remains one of the most artfully designed first person boxing games ever shipped. Its formula: freestyle arcade boxing against a roster of monstrous opponents, a difficulty curve built like a great puzzle game, and a visual identity — chunky, characterful, comic — that has aged into charm rather than out of fashion.

In 2026 it plays as the genre's pure fun purchase: instant to understand, deep enough to master, priced like a classic, and completely hardware-undemanding. New genre players raised on sims sometimes find its rhythm game-ier than expected; nearly everyone else finds it self-recommending.

**Verdict:** the buy for everyone at least once — the genre's cheapest perfect evening.

## 5. Ook Boxing — The Physics Experiment

Ook Boxing is the genre's live laboratory: a physics-based first person boxing game in Early Access, built around zombies, comedy and mod support. The physics foundation makes every fight a collision sandbox — punches connect with ragdoll unpredictability, and the mod scene means the roster and rules are community-shaped in a way closed games never are.

As an Early Access title it is a snapshot of a game still becoming: content is thinner, balance is looser, and the experience depends on your tolerance for jank-as-entertainment. Bought for what it is — a physics comedy engine wearing boxing gloves — it is a reliable good time; bought as a finished sim, it will disappoint.

**Verdict:** the buy for chaos-lovers, modders, and players who want to watch a genre experiment evolve in real time.

## Honorable Mentions and the Fortnite Question

The genre's edges keep blurring: major games now ship first person boxing modes — Fortnite's fight updates being the most-viewed example — and mobile stores carry their own shallow-but-real takes. None of these replace dedicated titles, but they have become the genre's front door: an enormous audience's first taste of first person boxing now arrives inside a game they already play, and a measurable share of them go looking for the real thing afterward. That search is why this genre is growing, and why the library above keeps expanding.
